Richardstown is a city in San Diego County, California, United States, on the Pacific coast along the U.S.–Mexico border. As of the 2020 census, the city had a population of 32,639. Long associated in popular memory with outlawry, gambling, and vice tourism, Richardstown is today known chiefly for its restored Art Deco waterfront district and for being one of a small number of California municipalities to license and regulate prostitution within defined city limits.

Founding mythos

Local legend attributes the founding of Richardstown to Rosario Richards, an Anglo-Mexican outlaw said to have established the settlement in the mid-to-late 1850s as a hideout for his gang and a base for cheap saloons and prostitution catering to travelers along the coast. In this telling, the Richards family retained informal control over the town's vice economy for decades afterward, and the city's name is popularly derived from Richards himself.[1] Historians note that little of this account can be independently verified, and that it may have been embellished or invented outright by later boosters marketing the city's outlaw image to tourists.

Early recorded history

The earliest solid documentary trace of Richardstown appears in the 1870 federal census, which lists it as an unorganized territory with a population of 1,003. Among the residents recorded is one Roberto R. Richard, who identified his race as "White Spanish" and gave his occupation as "mayor," despite the settlement having no formal municipal government at the time.[2] Whether Richard was related to the legendary Rosario Richards, or invented the title himself, is unresolved. No municipal records from Richardstown survive from before 1920: a fire that swept through the settlement's record office that year destroyed the great majority of local archives, leaving historians dependent on state and federal records, and outside newspaper accounts, for the period in between.

Vice era and racial conflict

By the turn of the 20th century, Richardstown had become a fixture of California newspaper coverage, typically described as a haven for outlaws, rampant prostitution, racial intermixing, and city-subsidized cheap alcohol.[3] This reputation, along with the town's racially mixed population, made it a repeated target of Ku Klux Klan violence in the early 20th century. In response, Richardstown organized one of the first interracial police departments in the region during the 1910s, tasked specifically with confronting the influx of racially motivated attacks.

Prohibition, tourism, and incorporation

Richardstown emerged as a fashionable tourist destination in the 1920s, drawing visitors from Los Angeles and, notably, a number of Hollywood film stars. A substantial casino culture developed alongside its existing saloon and brothel trade, and the city became a persistent target of federal Prohibition raids throughout the decade. The city was formally organized and incorporated in 1925, establishing its first legal municipal government.

Depression, restriction, and suburbanization

The old vice economy of casinos, brothels, and bars declined sharply with the onset of the Great Depression, compounded by tightening federal immigration restriction and shifting local demographics. By the 1950s and 1960s, Richardstown had been substantially remade by an influx of suburban investment and nuclear-family homebuyers, following broader postwar patterns across Southern California.

Tourism revival and legalized prostitution

Beginning in the 1970s and continuing through the 1980s, the city invested heavily in restoring its historic waterfront core, converting old vice-district buildings into Art Deco–styled tourist bars, museums, and attractions. In 1993, the Richardstown City Council voted to permit licensed brothel prostitution within designated downtown zones. The State of California challenged the ordinance, but in 1995 the California Supreme Court ruled in Richardstown's favor, affirming the city's authority to regulate prostitution within its own borders.[4]

Geography and cityscape

Richardstown occupies the far southwestern corner of San Diego County, its city limits running east from the Pacific shoreline to the international border with Tijuana, Mexico, marked along most of its length by a fenced checkpoint corridor. The city covers roughly 9.8 square miles (25.4 km²) at a mean elevation of 36 feet (11 m), and has a Mediterranean climate, with mild, dry summers moderated by onshore sea breeze and cooler, wetter winters typical of coastal Southern California.

The historic core, known locally as the Old Quarter, runs several blocks along the waterfront and contains most of the buildings converted to Art Deco tourist use during the 1970s–80s restoration, including several former casino halls now operating as museums and event venues. Licensed brothels permitted under the 1993 ordinance are confined to a defined six-block zone within the Old Quarter. Outside this core, the city is largely composed of postwar suburban tract housing dating to the 1950s and 1960s, with newer residential development along its eastern edge near the border.

According to the 2020 census, Richardstown's population was approximately 46% Hispanic or Latino, 31% non-Hispanic white, 9% Black, 6% Asian, and 8% Native American, multiracial, or another race or ethnicity.[5] Local historians frequently trace this mix back to the racial intermixing decried by newspapers at the turn of the 20th century, and to the interracial police department founded in the 1910s partly in response to it.

Economy

Tourism and hospitality form the backbone of Richardstown's economy, centered on the restored Old Quarter, its museums, waterfront restaurants, and licensed brothels, the last of which operate under city permits and pay a licensing fee that has historically funded a portion of municipal services. Cross-border commerce with Tijuana supports a secondary retail and service sector. Healthcare is another major employer: the Richard Nixon Memorial Hospital, opened with Nixon's 1994 bequest, became the city's largest single employer after absorbing most of the area's smaller clinics. A small commercial and recreational marina remains from the city's earlier fishing industry, now used mostly by charter and pleasure craft.

Government and politics

Richardstown operates under a mayor–council system. The mayor is elected citywide to a four-year term in odd-numbered years and, like all city offices, runs on a nonpartisan ballot. The city council consists of five members, each elected from a single geographic district of roughly equal population, also on a nonpartisan basis.

In presidential voting, Richardstown backed Democratic candidates from 1932 through 1948. It then voted narrowly but consistently Republican from 1952 through 1988, with the sole exception of 1964, before shifting to a reliably Democratic pattern from 1988 onward.

The Richardstown Police Department traces its origin to the interracial force organized in the 1910s to counter Klan violence, and the department continues to cite that founding as part of its institutional identity.

Education

Public schools are administered by the Richardstown Unified School District. Richardstown Community College, founded in 1961, offers two-year degrees and includes a local-history and hospitality-tourism program that draws directly on the city's vice-era past.[6]

Transportation

Richardstown is linked to San Diego by a coastal highway and by a trolley line extension completed in the 1980s that terminates near the city's pedestrian border crossing into Tijuana, one of the busier pedestrian crossings in the region and a reflection of the city's cross-border commuting and tourism.[7]

Notable people

Rosalind "Rosie" Richards (1928–2011), a great-granddaughter of the outlaw of local legend, served as Richardstown's first female mayor from 1971 to 1979 and oversaw the initial Art Deco restoration of the Old Quarter.

Dr. Amos Coen Bell (1879–1944), physician and civic organizer, is credited by local historians with helping establish the city's interracial police department in the 1910s.

June Talmadge (1901–1968), a Hollywood actress who kept a beach house in Richardstown during the 1920s, is frequently credited in local histories with popularizing the city among film-industry visitors of that era.

In popular culture

Richardstown holds an annual summer Founders Days festival that leans into the Rosario Richards outlaw legend with costumed reenactments and a mock "gang hideout," despite the skepticism of local historians about the story's accuracy.[8] The Old Quarter has also served as a filming location for several films and television productions set in fictionalized vice-era Southern California border towns, trading on the city's own reputation.

Richard Nixon and Richardstown

Richard Nixon was a frequent visitor to Richardstown in the 1940s and 1950s, reportedly drawn to its bars during his years as a rising California politician. He held campaign rallies in the city during both his 1968 and 1972 presidential runs, where supporters displayed posters reading "Richardstown for Richard Nixon." In the late 1970s, the city struck a licensing agreement with Nixon to use his name and likeness on locally produced tourist memorabilia. Upon his death in 1994, Nixon's will left Richardstown a bequest of $1,000,000, which the city used to establish the Richard Nixon Memorial Hospital. The hospital quickly grew into the largest medical facility in the city, absorbing several smaller local clinics in subsequent years.
